Mission

Supporting Vermont before the next outage.

Vermont's geography, weather, and aging infrastructure make it a strong fit for low-cost, decentralized communications. A mesh network can connect neighbors, volunteers, and responders even when the normal systems fail.

The first deployment centers on the University of Vermont campus, where a solar-powered RAK WisMesh Repeater Mini can become a durable backbone node for the surrounding community.

How it works

No towers, no internet, no subscription.

Meshtastic and MeshCore turn inexpensive LoRa (Long Range) radios into a decentralized, peer-to-peer mesh. Every device that hears a message rebroadcasts it, so the network's reach grows with every new node — no cell towers, no ISPs, and no monthly bill.

Each person carries a small radio paired to their phone over Bluetooth. Fixed, elevated repeater nodes stitch coverage together across a city or valley. When internet is available, a node can bridge the local mesh to the global Meshtastic network — when it's not, the radio mesh keeps working entirely on its own.

Meshtastic vs. MeshCore

Both are open-source LoRa mesh firmware, and both run on the same commodity hardware — they're not competitors so much as two different tools for different stages of a deployment.

Firmware Best for Notes
Meshtastic Community and grassroots deployment, getting started Larger community, more mature app ecosystem, flood routing
MeshCore Municipal or managed deployments, 50+ node scale Smarter routing, structured JSON telemetry, defined node roles

VERSO's approach: start with Meshtastic for the Burlington pilot, where community adoption and app support matter most — then evaluate MeshCore as the network scales toward a formal, multi-node municipal deployment.

Clients, routers, and repeaters

Every device on the mesh plays one of two roles. A client is the handheld radio someone carries, paired to their phone — it sends and receives messages and can relay a hop or two, but it isn't meant to carry the network. A router or repeater is different: a fixed node, usually mounted at elevation, whose main job is to rebroadcast every packet it hears so a message travels far past the range of any single device. Repeaters carry no GPS or telemetry chatter of their own — they exist purely to extend everyone else's signal.

LoRa radio also depends on line of sight. Hills, ridgelines, and dense tree cover attenuate or block the signal outright, so elevation is the single biggest lever for coverage — a repeater on a summit can "see" over terrain that would stop a valley-to-valley link cold, turning a 2–3km urban range into tens of kilometers.

Setting up a client

  1. Flash Meshtastic firmware and set your region to US
  2. In the app: Device Config → Role → Client (the default — no change needed for most people)
  3. Pair over Bluetooth and carry it — no external antenna or fixed power required
  4. Leave GPS on if you want to share your position with the mesh, or off to stay private

Setting up a router or repeater

  1. Mount at elevation with an external antenna, on wall or solar power
  2. In the app: Device Config → Role → Router (relays and can still message) or Repeater (relay only, doesn't appear on the map)
  3. Set a fixed position instead of tracking GPS movement
  4. If the site has internet, enable WiFi + MQTT so the node shows up on meshmap.net

Open source, top to bottom

Nothing about this network depends on a single vendor. Meshtastic and MeshCore firmware, the phone apps, and the communication protocol are all developed in the open, with source code anyone can read, audit, or modify. There's no proprietary radio to buy from one manufacturer and no subscription that could disappear if a company shuts down — any Meshtastic-compatible LoRa board from any maker works on the same mesh.

That matters for a public resilience project: the code Vermont Emergency Management or a future town partner would rely on during an outage is the same code anyone can inspect today. VERSO plans to publish its own deployment configuration, node placement research, and lessons learned on GitHub, so any other Vermont community can copy the approach without starting from scratch.

First node

Votey Building at UVM as a launch point.

Proposed deployment

The inaugural node will be placed at the Votey Building on the University of Vermont campus. A solar-powered RAK WisMesh Repeater Mini would be mounted on a pole to create a reliable, elevated communications relay for nearby neighborhoods and campus users.

  • Elevated placement improves range and line-of-sight
  • Solar power supports continuity during grid outages
  • Open-source hardware keeps the deployment affordable and adaptable

Why this matters

A single well-placed node can strengthen local coverage, help new users discover the mesh, and establish a credible foundation for future expansion into Burlington and beyond.

Already in motion: Two Heltec V4 client devices are already live on the mesh today — one carried by VERSO's director, one with the student team — gathering real coverage data around Burlington ahead of the first fixed node going in at Votey.

Redundancy and resilience

Mesh networks have already proven useful in real U.S. disasters.

September 2024 · Western North Carolina

Hurricane Helene

Helene's flooding washed out highways, closed mountain passes, and knocked out power for weeks. The FCC reported 3,432 cell sites down across the Southeast — in the hardest-hit counties, fewer than 10% remained operational. There was no pre-deployed mesh infrastructure before the storm; adoption was entirely organic, as people who already owned Meshtastic devices began using them in the days and weeks afterward. That grassroots response grew directly into NC Mesh, now building a statewide network of grid- and solar-powered radios.

What worked

  • Carried mutual-aid traffic for days — "family safe at shelter," "route 19 closed," "medic needed at 41st and Oak"
  • Solar-charged devices with large battery reserves ran for days without grid power
  • The mesh kept functioning as a standalone local network even with zero internet

What didn't work

  • Newcomers spamming status updates every 30 seconds briefly crowded out real emergency traffic
  • No pre-deployed backbone meant early adopters were isolated "island" nodes with no mesh to join
  • Most of the value arrived days to weeks later, once device density finally built up

Key lesson for Vermont: The infrastructure needs to exist before the disaster. A pre-deployed Burlington mesh with documented operating conventions would have been immediately useful during Irene and the 2023 floods — not gradually useful over weeks.

Sources: NC DPS, NC Mesh, FCC / telecom analysis, DEV Community case study

February 2021 – ongoing · Austin, Texas

Winter Storm Uri & Austin Mesh

Austin Mesh was founded in the wake of Winter Storm Uri, when widespread power failures left residents without electricity, heat, or communication for days. The goal from day one: a city-wide text-messaging layer that works without any external infrastructure — no power, no cell towers, no internet. Austin Mesh frames this explicitly as an equity problem — first responders have proprietary emergency systems, but ordinary citizens don't. A community-owned, open-source mesh with no subscription fees and sub-$100 devices closed that gap. It's now one of the most thoroughly documented mesh deployments in the country, covering roughly 2,600 square miles.

What worked

  • Solar-powered repeaters on high points provide backbone coverage that personal devices connect to, instead of requiring device-to-device links
  • A community-driven model with no central administration scaled organically as residents added nodes
  • Published, detailed operating conventions cover channel discipline, firmware selection, and node configuration

What didn't work

  • MQTT bridging is discouraged — busy MQTT servers can flood the whole network and choke local traffic
  • Hill-and-valley terrain often needs many relays; Meshtastic's default seven-hop ceiling constrains metro-scale coverage
  • Getting non-technical residents to follow operating conventions remains an ongoing challenge as the network grows

Key lesson for Vermont: Austin's published operating conventions and firmware comparisons are worth reading before finalizing Vermont's approach. Their move from Meshtastic to a hybrid Meshtastic/MeshCore architecture as the network scaled maps directly onto Burlington's likely growth path — no need to reinvent what Austin has already documented.

Sources: Austin Mesh, Austin Mesh — MeshCore vs. Meshtastic

2024–2025 · Texas flood events

Texas floods and mutual-aid response

The 2024 and 2025 Texas floods gave Austin Mesh's pre-deployed backbone a second, independent test. Because the repeater infrastructure already existed from the Winter Storm Uri build-out, community mesh operators didn't have to stand up a network mid-disaster — they simply used the one that was already there. Off-grid radio communications carried mutual-aid traffic for days at a stretch while commercial networks were degraded or unavailable.

What worked

  • Existing backbone meant flood response started immediately instead of waiting on new node density to build
  • Mutual-aid message patterns mirrored Helene's — status checks, road closures, resource locations, medical needs
  • The network kept operating even as cellular and internet service degraded across affected areas

What didn't work

  • Coverage was still bounded by however far Austin's existing repeater footprint reached
  • Public documentation of the flood response itself is thinner than Austin Mesh's general operating guides

Key lesson for Vermont: This reinforces the core lesson twice over — a backbone built before the emergency is what makes the network useful on day one of the next one, whether that emergency is a hurricane, an ice storm, or a flood.

Source: DEV Community case study

Questions

Frequently asked questions.

Do I need a ham radio license?

No. Standard Meshtastic and MeshCore operation on 915MHz falls under FCC Part 15 — the same unlicensed category as WiFi routers and Bluetooth devices.

Does the mesh work without cell service or internet?

Yes — that's the whole point. Devices talk directly to each other over LoRa radio and rebroadcast messages for one another, so the network keeps working even when cellular, internet, and grid power are all down.

What does it cost to join?

A personal handheld device runs $35–100. There's no subscription, no account, and no registration — install the free app, flash the firmware, and join the public BTV-Mesh channel.

Is my traffic private?

All channels use AES-256 encryption, and direct messages between two devices are end-to-end encrypted.

How can UVM students get involved?

This project is designed around ORCA (Open Research Community Accelerator) semester engagements, with open project areas in network deployment, GIS coverage modeling, public dashboards, and emergency knowledge systems. Reach out through VERSO's GitHub to get involved.

Is this actually open source?

Yes, top to bottom. The Meshtastic firmware, MeshCore, and the phone apps are all open source, and the hardware isn't tied to one manufacturer — any compatible LoRa board works. VERSO is publishing its own deployment configuration and research on GitHub as it's produced.

What's next

A corridor-first approach to statewide coverage.

VERSO is currently running GIS coverage analysis on potential repeater sites along Vermont's main population corridors — Burlington, Montpelier, Rutland, Brattleboro, and Bennington — using terrain and viewshed modeling to find where a single elevated node would do the most good before any hardware gets committed to a site.

Beyond UVM, we're exploring partnerships with municipal buildings and public libraries as deployment sites. Both are spread across nearly every Vermont town, already have power and roof or pole access, and carry a kind of public trust that private property doesn't.

For now, deployment stays intentionally ad hoc — nodes go up where a volunteer, partner, or research placement makes one possible, rather than waiting on a single master plan. But as the network grows past a handful of nodes, that approach needs a backstop. VERSO is working to bring together an organizing body — spanning VERSO, Green Mountain Mesh, Lab B, and municipal partners — that can make shared decisions about maintaining existing infrastructure and where new nodes go next.

Where we're focused

  • GIS viewshed analysis along the Burlington–Montpelier–Rutland–Brattleboro–Bennington corridor
  • Municipal buildings and public libraries as candidate host sites
  • Ad hoc placement for now — a partner or volunteer makes a site possible
  • Forming a shared organizing body for maintenance and node placement decisions

Where the mesh can fail

Risk Mitigation
A key corridor repeater loses power or goes offline Prioritize solar sites with oversized battery reserves, and place overlapping nodes along each corridor so one outage doesn't split the mesh
Hardware is damaged, stolen, or vandalized Favor secured municipal, library, or institutional sites over exposed public property, and keep per-node cost low enough that a loss isn't catastrophic
Channel congestion as adoption grows, crowding out real traffic Publish and enforce Vermont-specific operating conventions before public launch, modeled on Austin Mesh's documented approach
No one is responsible for a node once its installer moves on Stand up the organizing body early, with clear ownership and a maintenance plan attached to every site before it goes live
